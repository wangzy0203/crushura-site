Screen media compared: manganese, polyurethane, rubber

Home / News / Screen media compared: manganese, polyurethane, rubber

Manganese, polyurethane and rubber each fit a different duty. Match the material to the feed, then check tension, support beams and holes.

2026-10-09

Screen media usually comes down to a choice between three materials: manganese steel, polyurethane and rubber. Each of them is the right answer for one duty and the wrong answer for another. None of the three has replaced the others, and none of them wins on every screen. This article sets out what each material does well, how to match it to the feed in front of you, and three checks from the field that matter more to screen life than the choice of material itself.

Manganese steel

Manganese steel has the highest toughness of the three materials and resists impact well. That makes it the default choice for large lumps and coarse screening, where the feed arrives in big pieces and the deck takes the hit rather than an even bed of fines. Its open area is moderate — not the most open deck you can fit, not the least. The trade-off shows up with wear. As manganese steel wears, the openings deform, and after a spell in service the aperture is no longer the one the screen was ordered with. If your cut point has to stay tight as the media wears, that deformation is a cost you carry.

Polyurethane

Polyurethane screen media lasts about twice as long as ordinary manganese steel. That is the main reason buyers accept a price that is slightly higher. Polyurethane also resists blinding, so a wet, sticky feed that would peg the openings on a steel deck keeps passing through. Noise is low as well. It has one clear limit: polyurethane is not suitable for hot material. Where the feed runs hot, this material is out of the running before life or price is discussed. For everything else, the case for polyurethane rests on two things: how long it lasts, and how well it keeps the openings clear.

Rubber

Rubber resists impact well, and it is the quietest of the three materials. What it gives up is open area. A rubber deck has low open area, and fine screening efficiency drops with it. That does not make rubber a poor choice everywhere. It makes it the wrong choice where sharp separation at small apertures is the job, and the right choice at the end of the line, on finished-product screens, where impact and noise are the problems you are trying to solve. On a fine cut, that compromise shows up in screening efficiency.

How to choose

The three materials are not ranked from worst to best. They are matched to the duty in front of you. Work through the cases below against your own screen:

  • Large particle size and strong impact — manganese steel.
  • Wet, sticky feed that blinds the deck — polyurethane.
  • Finished-product screens, or a site where noise is a problem — rubber.

Where two cases apply at once, the material that solves your worst problem usually wins. Impact resistance, anti-blinding behaviour and low noise rarely come in the same deck, so there is always something to trade away.

Three things from the field

Material is only one variable in screen life. Three points come up on site again and again, and they apply whatever material is on the deck:

  • Tension. Screen tension affects service life more than the material does. Check it before you blame the media.
  • Support beams. If the beams are worn flat, replace the beams first, before new media goes on.
  • Holes. Replace a torn hole immediately. One hole is enough to let the finished product run coarse.

Sort out tension, beams and holes, and a correctly chosen screen has room to work. Skip them, and the same screen will fail early, whatever material it is made from.
